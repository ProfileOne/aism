awareness and strategic nous. We want you to interrogate the obvious, to question the
conventional wisdom, to ask the awkward questions. Public policy has a disturbing
tendency to become more complicated the less one knows about it.

SECTION 1: OVERVIEW OF THE COMMITTEE
The All India Stakeholders Meet (AISM) is a simulation of a multi-
stakeholder deliberative forum in which delegates from governmental,
political, media, digital, civil society and other agencies deliberate on
matters of national significance. Unlike traditional MUN committees,
delegates do not represent countries but a spectrum of stakeholders.
Delegates are expected to research their stakeholder’s portfolio,
interests, obligations and resolutions passed previously by them.
At AAWAAZ 2.0, AISM will operate under Unconventional Rules of
Procedure, as determined by the Executive Board, to promote robust
debate and discussion, including negotiations between stakeholders.
Delegates should keep in mind their stakeholder’s interests and work
towards evolving pragmatic and balanced solutions.
The agenda for this session - “Regulating Misinformation, Hate Speech
and Content Moderation while Preserving Freedom of Expression” seeks
to address one of the most pertinent issues of our times: The regulation
and moderation of digital space. Delegates will deliberate on issues
concerning misinformation, online hate speech, censorship, regulation,
moderation, and related issues while grappling with the challenge of
preserving freedom of speech and expression, and by extension
individual freedoms and democratic society itself.ALL INDIA STAKEHOLDER’S MEET ALL INDIA STAKEHOLDER’S MEET ALL INDIA STAKEHOLDER’S MEET

SECTION 2: INTRODUCTION TO THE AGENDA
The digital regulatory environment in the Republic of India underwent a
significant transformation between 2021 and August 2026. India is today
the world’s largest connected democracy, with almost a billion Internet
users. As a result, the digital space has become the new arena for
political, economic, and social contestation.
Moreover, the scale has produced deluge effects as disinformation and
abuse have taken on new levels and forms. “Synthetic information”
presents a serious set of challenges for electoral integrity and public
discourse. The manipulation of the social media space by both state and
non-state actors has led to artificial financial market fluctuations. The
core of this committee's agenda revolves around three intersecting
pillars:
1. Misinformation and Synthetic Media: How does a democratic state
control extremely realistic computer generated "fake news" and
"deepfakes" without the creation of a "Ministry of Truth" that
dictates what is and is not real?
2. Hate Speech and Algorithmic Amplification: Colonial-era penal codes
were written for physical mobs. How do we update these laws to
regulate algorithmic amplification of hate speech, dog-whistling, and
bots that circumvent definitions of incitement to violence?
3.Content Moderation and Intermediary Liability: Shouldn't massive
digital platforms be treated as passive "post offices" that are entirely
immune from liability (safe harbor), or do they act as publishers
that should be held criminally responsible for any unlawful content
that appears on their platforms during an extremely compressed
time window?

SECTION 3: CONSTITUTIONAL LAW 
Before you can discuss even one IT Rule or penal code, you have to know
the Constitution of India. Whatever resolution or directions you propose
before this committee must stand the test of constitutionality because if
not, the Supreme Court will declare it void.
Article 19(1)(a) Article 19(1)(a) Article 19(1)(a) 
Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution guarantees all citizens the right to
freedom of speech and expression, which is the cornerstone of Indian
democracy. However, it is a qualified right, which means that the right
is available to every citizen subject to reasonable restrictions. The
Constitution allows the state to impose reasonable restrictions on the
exercise of this right under certain specific grounds mentioned in
Article 19(2). These restrictions can be imposed only if they fall under
the specific grounds mentioned in this article. The following are the
grounds on which the right of citizens to freedom of speech and
expression can be reasonably restricted:
Sovereignty and integrity of India
Security of the State
Friendly relations with foreign States
Public order
Decency or morality
Contempt of court
Defamation
Incitement to an offense
The Public Order Threshold : Courts have consistently held that 'public
order' is not a synonym for 'law and order'. A ban on communication for
reasons of public order can only be imposed if a direct link (proximate
causation) between the communication and an imminent domestic
disturbance is established. 
The Misinformation Trap: this committee faces one persistent and
especially serious friction point: “fake news”/”falsity” is not explicitly
mentioned in Article 19(2).

SECTION 4: COURT CASES & CASE STUDIES
To win a debate in this committee, you must cite case law. The Supreme
Court is the ultimate referee, and understanding their historical rulings

Here are the mandatory precedents you must know in deep detail:
Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) 
This is the most famous digital rights case in Indian history. The Court
evaluated the Information Technology Act, 2000, specifically examining
state censorship powers versus free speech.
1. Striking Down Section 66A: The court was against the Section 66A as
it was unconstitutionally vague. They held that the term offensive is
very wide and that the drafters cannot possibly define it. The court
said that the very nature of such a law makes people refrain from
their legitimate rights under the Constitution and that it creates a
chilling effect on freedom of speech and expression.
2. Upholding Section 69A: On the other hand, the Court did not strike
down the government’s power to block a site altogether (Section 69A).
It upheld the order because it is reasonable with an appropriate
process: the request for blocking must be approved by a high-level
committee, include written findings, and include a hearing of the
content creator, if possible.

The Crux for Delegates (Section 79): The Court laid down “Safe Harbor.”
Specifically, the Court read down Section 79(3)(b) and said that an
intermediary (social media) could lose the exemption as provided

Decided in January 2023, this is a landmark judgment, which
significantly amends the very foundation of the Constitution. For
decades, it was held that only the Government (State) could be sued for
violating fundamental rights enshrined in Part III of the Constitution.
This ruling by the Constitution Bench of Supreme Court (4:1), however,

The IT Amendment Rules, 2026 The IT Amendment Rules, 2026 The IT Amendment Rules, 2026 The IT Amendment Rules, 2026
Notified on 10th Feb, 2026 (with effect from 20th Feb), these new rules
transition India from a “due diligence” based approach to an “active
moderation” approach.
Defining SGI: The government has come out with a very detailed legal
definition of SGI (Synthetically Generated Information) in rule 2(wa).
Audio-visual or audio/or video media, which is synthetically or
algorithmically generated or manipulated so as to appear as audio-
visual or audio or video media, is considered SGI. Note that text-only
content (including ChatGPT content) is explicitly excluded.
Mandatory metadata labeling: Hosts are required by law to embed
cryptographically secured, permanent metadata provenance trackers
(proof of synthesis) within the work itself. Significant Social Media
Intermediaries (SSMIs) also must obtain user consent for hosting such
content and technically verify such consent.
19Biggest change: The new rules massively reduce the time for action by
the platforms, removing any “technical impossibility” defense.

Simple imprisonment for up to 2 years, a fine, community service, or a

SECTION 10: GLOSSARY

entities.

SECTION 10: GLOSSARY
Over-The-Top (OTT) Platforms: Online streaming services that are
distributed over the Internet, rather than through traditional television
broadcasting. Examples include Netflix.
Safe Harbor (Section 79): The legal shield provided to digital
intermediaries, that protects them against being sued for copyright
infringement, defamation, or other violations of law that are committed
using their platforms. In exchange for this immunity, intermediaries
are obligated to take down user generated content as and when required
by law.
Sahyog Portal: The portal set up by I4C where law enforcement agencies
can submit takedown requests to intermediaries directly, effectively
by-passing the judicial process.
Significant Social Media Intermediary (SSMI): One of the new
classifications of social media platforms under the IT (Amendment) Act,
2021, that requires them to comply with a special set of norms.
Synthetically Generated Information (SGI): According to Rule 2(wa) of the
IT Rules 2026, any audio, video or audio-visual content that is created
using an algorithm and has the appearance of being created by a human
is considered synthetically generated information, with limited
exceptions for text. It must be noted that SGI does not explicitly refer to
text generated by algorithms.
Traceability (Rule 4(2)): A provision that obliges encrypted messaging
platforms to trace a message back to its originator, if the intermediary
receives a court order asking them to do so. This has been a much
debated provision in the context of encryption and privacy.
